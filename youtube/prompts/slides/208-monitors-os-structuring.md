## Slide 1: Dijkstra's Vision and Computing Crisis
- Edsger Dijkstra challenged fundamental assumptions about software reliability in the 1970s
- Observed systematic failures in large software projects exceeding budgets and missing deadlines
- Argued that programming complexity was growing exponentially beyond human comprehension
- Introduced the concept of "structured programming" as a solution to the software crisis
- Warned against treating computing as mere automation rather than a mathematical discipline

## Slide 2: Two Conflicting Philosophies
- View A: Optimists believing in AI and automated solutions to complexity
- View B: Realists (possibly pessimists) recognizing inherent limitations in human understanding
- The divide between mathematical rigor and practical engineering approaches
- COBOL as symbol of "natural language" programming dream that failed
- Dijkstra advocated for View B - accepting limitations and working within them

## Slide 3: The Failure of Natural Programming Languages
- COBOL promised to make programming accessible to non-programmers
- Business logic expressed in "plain English" proved to be an illusion
- Natural language ambiguity created more problems than it solved
- Programs became verbose without gaining clarity or correctness
- Dijkstra saw this as evidence that programming requires mathematical precision

## Slide 4: Software Projects and Budget Overruns
- Notorious pattern of projects exceeding time and budget constraints
- IBM's OS/360 as prime example of complexity explosion
- Brooks' Law: Adding programmers to late projects makes them later
- Lack of formal methods led to unpredictable development cycles
- Industry treated programming as craft rather than engineering discipline

## Slide 5: Dijkstra's "Drug" Metaphor for Computing
- Dijkstra literally called certain programming practices "like a drug"
- Referred to programmers becoming addicted to clever tricks and hacks
- The seductive nature of complex solutions over simple, correct ones
- Warning against intellectual masturbation in programming
- Advocated for discipline and restraint in system design

## Slide 6: Complexity Beyond Human Comprehension
- Modern systems too complex for any individual to fully understand
- Emergent behaviors that cannot be predicted from components
- The gap between what we can build and what we can verify
- Dijkstra's prediction that complexity would only accelerate
- Need for mathematical abstractions to manage incomprehensible systems

## Slide 7: Monitors as Structuring Primitive
- Monitors provide mutual exclusion and condition synchronization
- Encapsulation of shared resources with controlled access procedures
- Condition variables for thread coordination without busy waiting
- Higher-level abstraction than semaphores, reducing programming errors
- Foundation for modern concurrent programming constructs

## Slide 8: Artificial Intelligence and False Hope
- AI presented as silver bullet for software complexity
- Dijkstra skeptical of AI solving fundamental programming problems
- The illusion that machines could automatically generate correct programs
- Distinction between automation and understanding
- Warning against delegating thinking to machines

## Slide 9: Mathematical Foundations vs Engineering Practice
- Dijkstra insisted on mathematical proofs for program correctness
- Industry resistance to formal methods as "impractical"
- The tension between academic rigor and commercial pressures
- Structured programming as compromise between theory and practice
- Legacy of this debate in modern software engineering

## Slide 10: Dijkstra's Legacy and Modern Relevance
- View A continues with modern AI/ML approaches to programming
- View B manifests in formal verification and type systems
- The prophecy of incomprehensible systems realized in neural networks
- Monitors evolved into modern concurrency primitives
- His warnings about complexity more relevant than ever
